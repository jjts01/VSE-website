Redundancy: plan for the failure you'll actually get.

Everything that has gone wrong on our shows, ranked by how often, with the fix for each.

3 min read · Updated September 2026

Redundancy is insurance, and like insurance it's cheap compared with the claim. A second encoder and a bonded backup connection add a few hundred pounds to a production; the alternative is your all-hands going black in front of 3,000 employees. This is our working risk register. The things that actually break, in roughly the order they break.

1. Internet (the most likely failure by a distance)

What happens: venue line saturates, guest Wi-Fi throttles, an IT policy blocks the streaming port, or a contractor unplugs the wrong cable. The fix: a dedicated wired circuit for the stream, separate from everything else; a bonded cellular unit as the second path, tested on the day; and encoders that fail over automatically. Connectivity in depth.

2. Remote speaker links

What happens: the keynote's home broadband drops, their laptop updates, their kid starts a download. The fix: a tested backup route (phone dial-in at minimum), their slides run from the gallery so the story can continue, and a holding graphic with their name ready. For critical speakers: pre-record a version as insurance.

3. Encoder or production machine

What happens: software crashes, a machine overheats, a driver decides today's the day. The fix: two encoders running in parallel to the same destination or to a switchable backup ingest; a second production machine mirroring the primary on bigger shows; a hard rule that nothing on the show machines is updated in the 48 hours before the event.

4. Power

What happens: a circuit trips when catering plugs in the urn. The fix: UPS units on every critical machine (encoder, switcher, network switch, router) sized for at least 15 minutes; gallery on a separate circuit from front-of-house where the venue allows it.

5. Audio

What happens: a radio mic battery dies mid-keynote; the stream gets room PA feed and sounds like a swimming pool; a remote speaker's return causes echo. The fix: fresh batteries at every break, a backup handheld mic on stage, a dedicated stream mix with mix-minus returns, and a sound engineer whose only job is listening to the stream. Stream audio explained.

6. Platform and CDN

What happens: the event platform has an outage, or a corporate network blocks the player. The fix: stream to a second destination (an unlisted YouTube or Vimeo link) that can be emailed to attendees in minutes; test the platform on the client's own corporate network in advance.

7. Playback and content

What happens: a VT arrives in the wrong format, a slide deck has fonts missing, the file "was definitely in the folder". The fix: content deadline 48 hours before, everything transcoded to a house format and checked, no content accepted on USB on the morning without a test play.

8. People

What happens: a crew member is ill; the presenter is stuck on a train. The fix: crew depth on shows that matter (a second operator who could take over), a standby presenter briefed, and a run order that anyone can pick up because it's written properly.

Budget rule: redundancy on the internet path and the encoder is never optional on a show with an audience over 100. Everything else scales with what the event is worth to you.
