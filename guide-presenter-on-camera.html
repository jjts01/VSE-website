Presenting to camera: a room of one.

Executives who command a conference hall often freeze in front of a lens. How to set presenters up to look natural, read well and take direction on a streamed event.

3 min read · Updated September 2026

A stage gives a presenter an audience to play to. A camera gives them a black circle. The energy they'd naturally draw from a room has to be manufactured, and the technique — eyeline, framing, pace — is different from live presenting. Most of it can be taught in a 20-minute session before the show.

Eyeline: talk to the lens

The viewer is in the lens. A presenter who looks at the floor manager, the monitor or the room is, to the stream, looking away from every single viewer. Put a small mark on the camera, or a face-shaped sticker, and tell them to talk to that person. For hybrid events, agree the split: the presenter addresses the room for the room's moments and the lens for the stream's — and the gallery cuts accordingly.

Framing and movement

Presenters who wander drift out of frame and out of the lit area. Mark the floor: a spot for the opening, a spot for the screen, and the limits. Explain the shot sizes so they know when a gesture is in frame and when it isn't. Standing beats sitting for energy; leaning slightly forward reads as engagement.

Teleprompters

A teleprompter lets a presenter deliver a script to the lens without losing eyeline. It needs a script formatted for it (short lines, no dense paragraphs), an operator who follows the presenter's pace rather than forcing it, and a rehearsal — reading a prompter naturally is a skill. For executives who won't rehearse, bullet-point prompts on a confidence monitor beneath the lens are often better than a full script.

Energy and pace

Cameras drain energy; a delivery that feels slightly over the top to the presenter usually looks right on screen. Pace slower than conversation, pause after key points (the cut to the graphic needs a beat), and smile before the first word. Ask them to imagine one specific person watching on a laptop — it changes the delivery more than any other note.

Wardrobe

Solid mid-tones; no fine stripes, checks or tight patterns (moiré); nothing the same colour as the set; nothing pure white or pure black if it can be helped; no green in a green-screen studio. Matte fabrics. Remove lanyards and rattling jewellery — lav mics hear everything. Bring a spare shirt for a long day.

Cues from the floor

Agree a small set of signals with the floor manager: "you're on", "wrap up" (a rolling hand), "cut" (a flat hand), and the count into a VT. Tell presenters what will happen if a remote guest drops or the prompter fails — a presenter who knows the plan stays calm; one who doesn't improvises badly.

The 20-minute prep session

  1. Show them the shot on a monitor so they know what the viewer sees.
  2. Mark the eyeline and the floor positions.
  3. Run the first 60 seconds twice, with notes on pace and energy.
  4. Practise the handovers: to a VT, to a remote guest, to Q&A.
  5. Explain the failure plan and the cues.

Every presenter, however senior, benefits from the 20 minutes. The ones who "don't need it" are the ones who most do.
