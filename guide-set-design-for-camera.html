Set design for camera: build for the lens, not the room.

A stage that wows the delegates in the hall can look like a flat wall on a stream. The principles that make a set work through a camera.

3 min read · Updated September 2026

A camera is a two-dimensional, narrow-angled, unforgiving eye. It flattens depth, exaggerates clutter and notices things a human in the room never would. Designing a set for a streamed or hybrid event means designing for that eye first, and the room second.

Depth: layers beat backdrops

The single biggest improvement to any set on camera is separation between the presenter and the background. A presenter standing a metre in front of a flat wall looks pasted on. Two to three metres of distance, with something between (a lit panel, a plant, a piece of furniture, a second layer of set) gives the camera depth to render. Lighting can then treat the presenter and the background separately, which is where the "broadcast look" comes from.

Design to the camera positions

Decide the shots before the set: a wide, a mid on the presenter, a two-shot for interviews, an over-the-shoulder for panels. Then place the set elements where those shots see them. Logos that sit perfectly centred in the wide vanish behind the presenter's head in the mid; a screen that's readable from the room is a blown-out rectangle on camera unless its brightness is set for the lens.

Colour and contrast

Mid-tones and muted colours read well; pure white and pure black are difficult (white burns out, black swallows detail). Avoid fine patterns. Narrow stripes and small checks shimmer on camera (moiré). Give the presenter a colour that contrasts with the set: a navy suit against a navy backdrop is a floating head. Brand palettes designed for print often need calming for camera; our own brand navy works because it's lit with depth rather than used flat.

Screens on set

LED walls and monitors on set are wonderful when they're managed and awful when they aren't. Content on them must be designed for the camera's exposure, not for the room's eyes; typically the screen runs at 20–40% of its room brightness. Frame rates and refresh rates need matching to the camera to avoid flicker and banding. The backdrop guide goes deeper.

Branding placement

Think in shot sizes. The wide can carry a large brand element; the mid needs a smaller repeated element that survives beside a head; the close-up carries branding through lower thirds rather than set. A logo the audience sees in every shot, at the right size, is far more valuable than one enormous logo seen only in the opening wide.

Furniture and human scale

Low, wide seating photographs better than tall chairs: it keeps heads in frame and lets the camera see set behind. Tables hide hands and gestures; if you need one, keep it low and narrow. Presenters standing beat presenters sitting for energy. And leave space: a crowded set reads as clutter on camera even when it looks lush in the room.

Practicalities that get missed

  • Cable routes and power for lights, screens and cameras, planned before the set is built.
  • Floor: matte, not glossy. Reflections kill lighting.
  • A place for a presenter monitor and teleprompter that doesn't appear in shot.
  • Room for the camera positions themselves, including the wide, which needs distance.
  • Sound: hard flat surfaces make a set echo. Soft materials and broken-up walls help the audio as much as the picture.

Ask your set designer to draw the set from each camera position, not just from the audience. If they can't, involve the video HOD before the build.
