Remote speakers: the ten-minute brief that saves the show.

Your remote presenter is a one-person outside broadcast unit who has never done this before. Here's how to set them up to succeed.

3 min read · Updated September 2026

The weakest link in most virtual events isn't the platform or the encoder. It's the keynote speaker joining from a hotel room on Wi-Fi with a laptop microphone. Everything that follows is what we send to and check with every remote contributor, in the order that matters.

1. Connection first, everything else second

A wired Ethernet connection beats any Wi-Fi. If wired isn't possible, the speaker sits close to the router, and everyone else in the building stops streaming for the duration. We ask for a speed test from the exact spot they'll present from. We want at least 10 Mbps upload for a good HD contribution, and we want to know what happens when a second device joins. Phone tethering makes a good backup path, and we test it.

2. Audio is 80% of perceived quality

A wired headset or a USB microphone transforms a contribution; a laptop's built-in mic in an echoey room ruins it. Headphones are non-negotiable: without them the speaker's speakers feed back into their mic and the whole broadcast gets an echo. We send a short list of affordable USB mics and, for keynote-level speakers, courier a kit.

3. Camera, framing and light

Camera at eye level: a stack of books under the laptop is fine. Light in front of the face, not behind it; a window in front of them is the best free key light there is. Frame with a little headroom, eyes in the top third. The background should be tidy and, ideally, real. Virtual backgrounds flicker at the edges and look like exactly what they are.

4. The environment

Door closed, notifications off, phone on silent, a note on the door. If they're presenting from an office, ask IT to whitelist the contribution platform in advance. Corporate firewalls are the most common cause of "it worked at home".

5. Rehearse on the real kit

Every remote speaker gets a technical check on the same laptop, in the same room, with the same connection they'll use live. A "quick test from my phone" tells us nothing. The check covers audio levels, framing, screen-share if they're presenting slides, and the return feed so they can see and hear the show.

6. Slides: take them off the speaker's machine

Wherever possible we run presenters' slides from the gallery, on cue from the speaker. It removes screen-share bandwidth, prevents notification pop-ups on screen, and lets us hold the last slide if their connection drops. The speaker sees a return feed of their slides and simply says "next".

7. The night-before email

Join link, join time (30 minutes before their slot), the phone number of a human in the gallery, a one-line reminder of the kit checklist, and what to do if the link fails, usually a dial-in phone number as a last resort so their voice at least can be heard over a holding slide.

Rule of thumb: if a speaker hasn't been tested on their real setup, assume they will fail, and have a plan for it in the run order.
